2 · The page copy, final

Verbatim into the build. Structure markers (H1, section, room) describe hierarchy, not visual treatment.

Page header

H1

AI, trust and communications

Lead

Your organisation is already using AI, whether or not the board has decided to. Someone on your team pasted something into a chatbot this week. Across the sector, adoption moves at the speed of a free trial and governance hasn't kept up. For values-led organisations the stakes run higher than most: you hold sensitive data by definition, you run on trust, and there's no technical team to hand the problem to.

None of this needs to be alarming. It needs to be governed. The organisations that do this well get more from the tools, with less risk, because someone has made a real judgement about where AI belongs in the work and where it doesn't. That judgement is what I do.

I use these tools every working day, and I'm honest about them: excited by what they make possible, clear-eyed about what they cost.

Section one · Governance

H2

AI your organisation can answer for

Body

I work with chief executives, senior teams and trustees on adopting AI responsibly and being able to answer for it. That starts with finding where AI is already in use, including the tools nobody approved, because you can't govern what you haven't found. From there: a policy short enough that people actually read it, data matched to the sensitivity of whatever tool touches it, and a plan for AI-enabled reputational harm decided before you need it, not drafted mid-incident.

Most AI governance is written for organisations with in-house counsel and a technical team. You probably have neither. So I build governance a non-specialist can run: one page rather than forty, a named owner rather than a committee, and a board that can see what's in use, who owns it, and what would happen if it failed.

Pull-out line

A policy that lives in a shared drive and shapes no behaviour is governance theatre. Communication is what makes it real, and communication is my trade.

Section two · Training

H2

Training built on your team's real work

Body

I train senior teams, whole staff, and boards. Most AI training makes a room feel busy for a day and changes nothing by Friday. Mine is built on your team's actual tasks, gathered through a short diagnostic before anyone walks in, and it carries the honest version: what these tools do brilliantly, what they cost, and where they don't belong.

The four rooms

For leadership teams. The working understanding leaders need to set direction and govern adoption, without touching a tutorial. A board version closes with a written summary for trustees.

For the whole team. From a hands-on half day to a full day together. Prompts written as professional briefs on real tasks, tested live, and every person's setup configured before they leave.

For chief executives, one to one. A short private series for leaders who would rather build fluency on their own work, confidentially, than in a room with their team.

For the people who are accountable. Data handling matched to real sensitivity, the difficult scenarios argued out in the room, and a drafted AI-use policy in your hands at the end.

Section close

Every session ends with something configured, something committed to, and a check-in already in the diary. Sceptics are welcome in every room; most of their objections are right. The exact shape of a session gets worked out at proposal stage, once I've seen the diagnostic.

Recent rooms include a leadership session for a multi-site community organisation and a private fluency series with its chief executive.

Closing CTA

H2

Where to start

Body

If you don't know which of these you need, that's normal. The discovery call exists to work it out: 30 to 45 minutes, no preparation needed. A rough sense of what the organisation is dealing with is enough. Pricing is scoped per engagement and arrives at proposal stage.

4 · Guard rails, do not breach

  • The copy is final. Claude Code proposes design, never wording. Any copy problem found during build comes back to Jasmin, it does not get fixed in the editor.
  • No pricing anywhere. The public line is in the CTA copy and goes no further.
  • No employer named. The proof line's anonymisation ("a multi-site community organisation", "its chief executive") is locked wording.
  • Sector-neutral. The About sentence is the only Jewish communal reference added anywhere in this build. The AI page itself stays sector-neutral.
  • The homepage hero is untouchable. Headline, subline, layout, all of it.
  • Named training products stay off the site. The four rooms are the public framing; product names surface at proposal stage.
  • Preview only. Nothing merges to main without Jasmin's review of the preview URL.

5 · Definition of done

The build is done when: the page is live on a Vercel preview URL with all five navigation changes in place; the design reads as native next to the existing pages; the page meets the accessibility and contrast standard the rest of the site meets; it holds up at mobile widths; every new link resolves; and Jasmin has reviewed the preview and approved the merge.

After merge, one verification pass on production: read the whole site through as a cold visitor for register consistency, and confirm the new links resolve on the live domain.
